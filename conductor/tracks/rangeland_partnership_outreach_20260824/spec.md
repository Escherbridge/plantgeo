# Rangeland partnership and feedstock-policy outreach

Chartered 2026-08-24. **Non-software** — ships no code, owns no source files. Recorded here so
the relationships and deadlines are not lost in a chat log. See `conductor/RUNBOOK.md` §0.41.

## 1. Technique fit — settle this before any conversation

Four techniques were assessed against this biome (8–12″ precipitation sagebrush steppe, growing-
season NDVI ~0.17 at the reference point against a regional mean of 0.48):

| technique | verdict |
|---|---|
| **Silvopasture** | **Poor fit.** Integrates *trees* with grazing; the site will not carry canopy without irrigation. No Great Basin steppe feasibility literature exists. |
| **Agroforestry** | **Largely inverted.** Western juniper expanded ~4× in Oregon (600 k ha in 1930 → >2.6 M ha). Agencies fund **removal**. |
| **Biochar** | **Strong fit** — precisely *because* juniper is removed: the slash is the feedstock, and biochar doubles as the nitrogen-immobilisation lever below. |
| **Vegetation management** | **Core activity already.** Juniper removal, annual-grass control, fuel breaks, targeted grazing, post-fire seeding. |

**Do not open with agroforestry.** In this landscape it reads as working against the region's
stated conservation objective.

## 2. The nitrogen tension — the non-obvious one

Nitrogen enrichment **favours cheatgrass**, and cheatgrass itself raises soil N: a self-reinforcing
loop ending in more fire. The established counter-move is carbon addition to **immobilise** N.

So within a single "grazing + manure + biochar" pitch, two levers pull opposite ways:

- **Grazing (biomass removal)** — works, already deployed (BLM targeted grazing on the Owyhee
  Front cuts cheatgrass to ≤2″ before peak fire season).
- **Manure (nutrient loading)** — likely works *against* the goal on the invasion pathway.
- **Biochar** — works, and this is a second independent argument for it beyond slash disposal.

## 3. The greenstrip convergence

The independently-proposed "non-dry, low-oil plant species" idea is the established **greenstrip**
design criterion: high moisture content and low volatile oil reduce ignition probability, and the
mechanism is **disrupting fuel continuity**. RUNBOOK §0.41.3 found that same threshold in our own
data — burn rate jumps 8.1 % → 63.9 % from sparsest to middle cover within a dryness band.

Workhorse species is forage kochia (*Bassia prostrata*). **Counterevidence to carry honestly:** a
recent study found little forage kochia persisting in historically seeded fuel breaks in the
Northern Great Basin. Establishment is the hard part, not the concept.

## 4. Land tenure

BLM holds **61 % of Harney County**. Burns District 3.36 M acres; Vale District 5.1 M, with the
Malheur Field Office ~4.6 M. Practically: **BLM holds the surface, a grazing permittee holds the
operating interest, and a collaborative holds the relationships.** Work moves through the third.
Also present: private ranch land (the NRCS channel), Oregon DSL parcels, and Burns Paiute Tribe
interests — Tribes are independently *eligible applicants*, not merely consultees.

## 6. The biochar gap — sharpened

Not "the PNW lacks biochar". US production ran 86,000 t (2023) → 151,000 t (2025), and there is
active PNW commercial and Verra-certified work. The gap is narrower and more fixable:

- **Geographic** — PNW biochar is *westside forest-slash* oriented; interior rangeland/juniper is
  unserved. No biochar programme surfaced in either the WA Columbia Basin or the ID Snake River
  Plain, which is exactly where the highest-risk unburned cells sit.
- **Structural, and the real one** — the named industry bottleneck is *improving the terms of
  biomass procurement from federal lands*. In country that is 61 % BLM, that is the binding
  constraint, and it is a policy ask with a named owner rather than a technology problem.
- **Economic** — $500–800/ton and a low-density product that is expensive to move, which argues
  for processing at the slash pile and applying nearby, not a central plant.
